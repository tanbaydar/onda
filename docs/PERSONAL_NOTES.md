# Personal Notes

Why option 3 is the trap — and rejecting it is itself the interview answer. On-demand fetching puts an unofficial third-party endpoint inside the synchronous user request path. It breaks the firewall rule (the app now knows RA exists), makes search latency hostage to RA's response time and rate limits, and turns "RA blocks us" from catalog goes stale into search breaks in front of users. Every invariant we wrote exists to prevent exactly this shape. When an interviewer asks "why not fetch on demand?" — that paragraph is the answer, and it's a better signal than any feature.

But I want to force the consistency question the recommendation glides past, because an interviewer would: doesn't email reservation at registration recreate the very denial-edge I just used to kill UNIQUE(new_email)? Someone registers with your address, abandons it unverified — you now can't register until the purge runs. Same shape: an unverified claim blocking a legitimate owner. If we rule differently here, we owe the record a reason, and the recommendation's identity-vs-proposal distinction is right but incomplete. The full argument:

The alternative is worse in kind, not degree. Duplicate unverified emails create identity ambiguity — two account rows, one address, and verification/recovery must then pick a winner by some tiebreak, which is a corruption class. The email-change case never had this: pending proposals could coexist because DANCED_USER.email remained the single verified truth underneath them all. At registration there is no underneath — the row is the identity, so the address must be exclusive from birth. Different structural position, different ruling. That's the defensible line.

But the denial-edge still deserves its mitigation, and it's cheap: eager reclaim. Don't make the rightful owner wait for the nightly purge — at registration time, if the email belongs to an account that is unverified and expired, purge it inline and proceed with the new registration. The denial window collapses from "until cleanup runs" to "until the token actually expires," which is the honest minimum: while a token is live, the first registrant has a fair pending claim; after expiry, they've abandoned it. Annotate this as the registration flow's rule.
