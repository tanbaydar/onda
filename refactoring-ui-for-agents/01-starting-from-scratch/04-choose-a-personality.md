# Choose a Personality

## Use this module when

- Establishing a product’s visual direction.
- Selecting or changing typography, color, radius, imagery, or interface tone.
- A UI is internally neat but feels generic or contradictory.
- Different components imply different emotional qualities.

## Objective

Translate intended product character into a small set of consistent, concrete decisions instead of applying vague branding adjectives after implementation.

## Rules

1. Personality must serve the audience and product context.
2. Express personality through coordinated choices: typography, color, shape, imagery, spacing/density, and language.
3. Consistency matters more than novelty. Contradictory signals create amateur results.
4. Reference adjacent products used by the audience, but do not imitate a direct competitor so closely that the product loses identity.
5. A personality change is a system-level decision, not a local component tweak.

## Procedure

### 1. Define bounded traits

Choose two or three desired qualities and one or two explicit anti-traits. Prefer paired constraints:

- Calm, not inert.
- Formal, not hostile.
- Playful, not childish.
- Technical, not mechanical.
- Luxurious, not ornamental.

Avoid unbounded labels such as “modern” or “beautiful.”

### 2. Map each trait to visual channels

Record intended consequences:

| Channel | Decision questions |
|---|---|
| Typography | Neutral or expressive? Serif or sans? High or moderate contrast? Where is display type permitted? |
| Color | Restrained or energetic? Warm or cool? How many accents? What semantic jobs do they own? |
| Shape | Square, lightly rounded, or strongly rounded? Are exceptions semantic? |
| Imagery | Documentary, polished, illustrative, user-generated, abstract, or absent? |
| Density | Airy, compact, or context-dependent? Which surfaces require data density? |
| Language | Formal, direct, warm, playful, or technical? Which tone is prohibited? |

### 3. Check signal compatibility

An austere type system, playful large radii, saturated candy colors, and formal legalistic copy may each work separately but conflict together. Remove or change the weakest signal until the system reads as one product.

### 4. Define limits and exceptions

Specify where expressive type, accent color, large imagery, unusual shape, and informal language may appear. Scarcity creates identity; unrestricted use creates noise.

### 5. Validate with representative surfaces

Test at least one dense utility surface, one content-rich surface, one empty state, and one high-attention action. Personality must survive beyond a landing page.

## Failure modes

- Adding a new typeface to make one component “special.”
- Mixing square, lightly rounded, pill, and highly rounded controls without semantic roles.
- Using unrelated accent colors because individual screens look bland.
- Copying the closest competitor’s exact typography, palette, and layout grammar.
- Expressing luxury through empty space that reduces function.
- Treating tone of voice as independent from visual character.

## Verification

- An agent can describe the product in two or three traits and point to concrete system decisions for each.
- Every strong visual channel has a defined job and usage limit.
- Components do not contradict the chosen shape or tone conventions.
- Dense, empty, and transactional states still feel like the same product.
- References informed principles, not copied compositions.
- Any personality-changing choice has explicit product authority.
